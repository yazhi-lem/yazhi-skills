A reference is the cheapest credibility a company can buy, and the easiest to lose. The failure is rarely the ask; it is using a quote in a context the customer did not approve, or publishing a result the customer's legal team never saw. This skill owns the reference programme: who to ask, what to ask for, and how to use it without breaking the relationship.

## Reference types

| Type | Ask | Approval needed | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logo | "May we list you as a customer?" | Written, from the customer's marketing owner | Low |
| Quote | One or two sentences, attributed | Written, with the exact wording | Low |
| Case study | 1-2 pages, metrics included | Written, plus the metrics' owner | Medium |
| Reference call | A 30-minute call with a prospect | Per-call consent, not blanket | Medium |
| Public webinar | Joint presentation | Written, plus the customer's comms team | High |

## Workflow

1. **Qualify before asking.** A reference works when the customer got a measurable result and is willing to be named. A happy customer with no metric is a quote, not a case study.
2. **Ask for the smallest commitment that serves the need.** A logo is easier to approve than a case study; start there and grow the relationship.
3. **Get the exact wording approved in writing.** A verbal "sure, use my quote" is not approval; send the final text and keep the reply.
4. **Have the customer's metric owner confirm the numbers.** Marketing approves the story; the metric owner approves the number, and they are rarely the same person.
5. **Never round a metric upward.** "Cut onboarding time 40%" is a claim; "cut it 40% in our pilot" is the honest version. An overstated case study is a legal and reputational risk.
6. **Set an expiry on the reference.** Metrics and job titles age; a two-year-old case study misrepresents the customer today. Review annually or on request.
7. **Keep the reference's context attached.** A quote about a specific pilot should not be reused as a general product endorsement; keep the scope in the same sentence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Publishing a quote without the approved wording.** The customer's legal team sees a paraphrase they never agreed to; the relationship pays for the credibility.
- **Rounding a metric upward.** The fastest way to lose a reference and create a legal problem; the customer's own numbers are the only defensible ones.
- **Reusing a pilot quote as a general endorsement.** Strips the scope and overstates the result; keep the context in the sentence.
- **Asking for a case study as the first request.** A high-effort ask from a new customer reads as extraction; start with the logo.
- **No expiry.** A stale reference silently misrepresents the customer and the product; date it and review it.
- **Treating a reference call as a sales call.** The customer is doing the company a favour; a hard sell burns the reference and the relationship.
