Channel and partnership strategy answers "who else can put this product in front of a buyer, and at what cost to us." It's tempting to say yes to any partnership offer that shows up, but each one carries real integration, legal, and ongoing-management cost — the right question is never "is this partner interested," it's "does this partner's audience overlap with our ICP enough to justify the overhead."

## Channel types and what they actually cost

| Channel type | Revenue mechanism | Typical overhead | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct sales | Owned relationship, full margin | Highest per-deal cost, most control | Complex/high-ACV sales |
| Reseller / VAR | Partner sells, takes a margin cut (20-40%) | Enablement + margin cost, less control | Reaching markets/geos you can't staff directly |
| Technology / integration partner | Co-sell via product-to-product integration | Engineering + ongoing API maintenance | Products that are stronger together (data, workflow adjacency) |
| Marketplace listing (cloud, app store) | Discovery + simplified procurement | Marketplace fee (typically 3-20%), listing maintenance | Buyers who already procure through that platform |
| Affiliate / referral | Pay-per-conversion, low commitment | Low overhead, lower per-partner volume | Long-tail reach, low-touch products |
| Co-marketing (no revenue share) | Shared audience exposure | Coordination time, brand-alignment risk | Adjacent, non-competing audiences |

## Workflow

1. **Score partnership fit before any deal conversation**: audience overlap with your ICP, whether the partner's customers have the specific problem you solve, and whether the partner's incentive (their revenue, their retention) actually aligns with pushing your product rather than a competitor's.
2. **Decide the channel type before negotiating specific terms** — a reseller deal and a co-marketing deal have completely different overhead and success metrics; conflating them mid-negotiation produces a contract that doesn't fit either model cleanly.
3. **Model the fully-loaded cost before agreeing to a margin split** — integration engineering time, ongoing API maintenance, partner enablement, and deal-desk overhead all reduce the effective margin below the headline split.
4. **Write a joint success metric into the agreement**, not just a revenue target — a specific number of joint customers, a co-marketing reach number, or an integration adoption rate. A partnership with no measurable target drifts into "we talk quarterly but nothing ships."
5. **Start with a pilot scope (one region, one segment, a fixed time window) before a broad exclusive agreement** — exclusivity locks you out of better options before you have evidence this partner actually converts.
6. **Assign a single internal owner per partnership** who is accountable for enablement, escalation, and the quarterly business review — partnerships with no named owner decay silently until someone notices the pipeline never showed up.
7. **Build a partner enablement kit** (a scoped version of `sales-enablement`'s artifacts) before asking a partner's sales team to sell your product — an unequipped partner rep sells your product worse than they'd admit, and it reflects on you, not them.
8. **Set a review cadence with an exit clause you'll actually use.** Review partnership performance against the joint metric quarterly, and have a real off-ramp (non-renewal, deprioritization) for partnerships that aren't producing — sunk-cost thinking keeps dead partnerships on the books for years.

## Anti-patterns

- **Signing every inbound partnership request** without an ICP-overlap check — spreads enablement and management bandwidth across partnerships that were never going to convert.
- **Granting exclusivity before a pilot proves the channel works** — locks out better partners in that segment/region for the exclusivity term, often 1-2 years, based on hope rather than data.
- **Treating a technology integration as "done" at launch** — API versions drift, and an unmaintained integration silently breaks for joint customers unless someone owns its upkeep.
- **Measuring partnership success by activity (meetings held, co-marketing posts published)** instead of outcome (joint pipeline, joint revenue, integration adoption) — activity metrics flatter a partnership that isn't actually working.
- **Leaving a partnership un-owned internally** — no accountable person means enablement goes stale, escalations stall, and the relationship quietly dies without anyone deciding to end it.
