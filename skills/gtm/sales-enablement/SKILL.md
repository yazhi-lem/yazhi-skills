name: sales-enablement
description: Use when preparing a sales or customer-facing team to sell a new product, feature, or pricing change — building a pitch deck, battlecard, objection-handling guide, or onboarding a new rep. Helps you avoid shipping enablement material that reps skim once and never use because it doesn't match how a real sales conversation actually goes.
---

Sales enablement fails most often not from missing content but from content shaped like a marketing deck instead of a conversation aid — a wall of feature bullets nobody can recite from memory mid-call. The material that actually gets used is short, structured around what the rep will be asked, and rehearsed before it ships, not just distributed.

## Core enablement artifacts

| Artifact | Purpose | Refresh trigger |
|---|---|---|
| One-page narrative / pitch outline | The story a rep tells in the first 5 minutes | New positioning, new segment focus |
| Battlecard | Head-to-head vs. named competitors: strengths, weaknesses, landmines | New competitor, competitor feature parity, lost-deal pattern |
| Objection-handling guide | Top 8-10 objections with a one-line response each | Every QBR, from live win/loss data |
| Discovery question bank | Qualifying questions mapped to buyer pain points | New segment or ICP change |
| Demo script / flow | What to show, in what order, and why | Product UI change, new killer feature |
| Pricing/negotiation guardrails | Discount authority levels, when to escalate | Pricing change (see `pricing-strategy`) |

## Workflow

1. **Interview 3-5 reps (or do the discovery calls yourself) before writing anything** — the objections and questions that show up in real conversations are rarely the ones product marketing predicts from a desk.
2. **Write the one-page narrative first, everything else second.** If the core story doesn't fit on one page a rep can internalize in 10 minutes, no amount of supporting collateral will fix it.
3. **Build the battlecard around the actual competitors reps hear in deals**, not the ones leadership worries about strategically — pull this from CRM competitor-mention fields or recent loss reasons, not assumption.
4. **Write objection responses as a single sentence a rep can say out loud**, not a paragraph they'd have to paraphrase live — test it by having a rep read it cold in a role-play and see if it sounds natural.
5. **Run a live certification, not a passive read-and-sign.** Have each rep role-play the pitch and handle 2-3 objections in front of a manager or peer before they're considered enabled — a Slack link to a doc is not enablement.
6. **Tie every enablement asset to the current positioning statement and messaging pillars** from `positioning-and-messaging` — a battlecard that contradicts the public messaging creates confusion when a prospect compares a rep's claim to the website.
7. **Set a refresh cadence, not a one-time ship.** Battlecards and objection guides go stale within a quarter as competitors ship and buyers push back on new things; assign an owner and a quarterly review, not "update when someone complains."
8. **Instrument usage** — track which enablement assets reps actually open (most CMS/enablement platforms report this) and interview the reps who aren't using something before assuming the content itself is fine.

## Anti-patterns

- **Writing enablement material from the product spec instead of from real sales conversations** — produces a feature-complete but conversationally useless document.
- **Shipping a battlecard with no update owner** — it's accurate on ship day and wrong by the next competitor release, and nobody notices until a rep loses a deal citing stale information.
- **Treating enablement as a one-time training session** instead of an ongoing certification and refresh cycle — new hires get it once, tenured reps never revisit it, and both drift from current messaging.
- **Writing objection responses that a rep would never actually say out loud** — overly formal or jargon-heavy language gets abandoned in the first live call under pressure.
- **Skipping the live role-play/certification step** — assuming a rep who read the doc can execute it live under pressure is the single biggest gap between "enabled on paper" and "enabled in practice."
