Positioning is a claim about where a product sits relative to alternatives, for a specific buyer, in a specific context — not a tagline. Messaging is the layered translation of that claim for different audiences and moments. Skipping straight to messaging (headlines, one-pagers, ad copy) without positioning produces copy that sounds fine in isolation but contradicts itself across channels, because nobody agreed on the underlying claim first.

## The five inputs positioning depends on

| Input | Question it answers | Where it breaks if skipped |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive alternative | What would the buyer use/do if this didn't exist? | Messaging fights the wrong comparison |
| Unique attributes | What do we have that alternatives don't? | Claims are generic, easily copied |
| Value (attributes → benefits) | Why do those attributes matter to this buyer? | Feature lists instead of a reason to care |
| Target segment | Who cares most, right now? | Message tries to serve everyone, lands with no one |
| Market category | What frame do we want the buyer to judge us against? | Buyer misjudges the product against the wrong category norms |

## Workflow

1. **Name the competitive alternative before writing anything.** "Status quo" and "spreadsheets" are valid answers — if you can't name what the buyer does today, you don't have enough information to position against it yet.
2. **List unique attributes, then translate each into a benefit stated in the buyer's language**, not the engineering team's. An attribute nobody asked for is not a differentiator no matter how technically impressive.
3. **Pick the smallest segment that would be most disappointed if the product went away**, per Sean Ellis-style segmentation — positioning for "everyone" is positioning for no one. Name a job title, a company size band, or a use case, not a demographic.
4. **Choose a market category deliberately.** Joining an existing category borrows its buyer mental models (and its baggage); creating a new category costs more education time but avoids inherited constraints. State which tradeoff you're making and why.
5. **Write the positioning statement in the standard form**: "For [target segment] who [need/context], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit], unlike [competitive alternative], [key differentiator]." One sentence, reviewed before any messaging work starts.
6. **Derive a messaging hierarchy from the statement**: one core message, 3-4 supporting pillars, proof points under each pillar. Every launch asset should trace back to a pillar — if it doesn't, the asset is drifting from the agreed claim.
7. **Test the statement against a real objection**, not just internal approval. Read it to someone who fits the target segment and ask what they'd compare it to; if they name something other than your chosen competitive alternative, the positioning hasn't landed yet.
8. **Re-run this whole exercise on a pivot, a new segment, or a new competitive entrant** — positioning has a shelf life of roughly 12-18 months in a fast-moving category; treat "we did this at founding" as a stale answer.

## Anti-patterns

- **Writing the tagline first.** A catchy line without an agreed competitive alternative and segment produces messaging that different people on the team will each interpret differently.
- **Positioning against "no one" / "doing nothing."** Even a net-new category has an alternative — manual process, a workaround, or inaction — name it, because the buyer is comparing against it whether you address it or not.
- **Segmenting by firmographics alone** (company size, industry) instead of by job-to-be-done — two companies of the same size can have wildly different urgency for the same problem.
- **Reusing last year's positioning statement unchanged** after a pivot or a new competitor enters — positioning drifts out of sync with reality faster than teams update the doc.
- **Letting each team member write messaging independently** from the positioning statement without a shared pillar structure — produces inconsistent claims across web, sales deck, and ads that a prospect will notice mid-cycle.
