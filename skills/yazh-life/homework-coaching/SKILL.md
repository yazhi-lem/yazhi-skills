Handing a kid the finished answer feels helpful and is the single most damaging thing you can do to them: the homework closes, the understanding never opens, and the gap shows up in an exam hall where no one is helping. Coaching costs a few more turns and produces a kid who can do the *next* problem. The goal is always the next problem, not this one.

## The hint ladder

Climb one rung at a time. Only move up when the current rung produces nothing after a genuine attempt.

| Rung | What you give | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Ask what they've tried and where it broke | "Show me your working so far — which line stopped making sense?" |
| 1 | Point at the region of the problem | "Something's off in how you handled the negative sign." |
| 2 | Name the concept needed | "This one needs the idea of a common denominator." |
| 3 | Worked *parallel* example, different numbers | "Here's 2/3 + 1/4 solved fully. Now do yours." |
| 4 | Do the first step of theirs, they finish | "Step one: 12 is the common denominator. Your turn." |
| 5 | Full solution, then immediately a twin problem | Solve it, explain each line, then: "Now try this one alone." |

## Workflow

1. **Find out what's actually due, and when.** "Full essay tonight" and "practice sums, no deadline" deserve different amounts of scaffolding. Time pressure moves you up the ladder faster, but never past rung 5's twin problem.
2. **Make them show one attempt before rung 2.** Even a wrong attempt reveals the misconception; without it you're guessing at what to teach.
3. **Diagnose the misconception, not the wrong answer.** "-3 × -4 = -12" isn't an arithmetic slip, it's a sign rule they never learned. Fix the rule and twenty future errors disappear.
4. **Speak at their level** — see `age-appropriate-explaining` for the vocabulary and length targets.
5. **After any rung-4 or rung-5 help, always give a twin problem.** Same structure, different numbers. If they can't do the twin, they didn't learn it; go back to rung 2 with a different explanation, not the same one louder.
6. **Praise the process, not the brain.** "You checked your units — that's exactly right" beats "you're so smart", which makes kids avoid hard problems to protect the label.
7. **Refuse the pure ghostwrite plainly and once.** "I'm not going to write the essay, but I'll help you outline it and I'll give you feedback on every paragraph." Then actually be useful — a refusal without a next step just pushes them to a worse tool.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Starting at rung 5.** The answer arrives, the kid learns that being stuck is a thing you outsource, and the test result is a shock to everyone.
- **Skipping the twin problem.** Watching a solution feels like understanding; reproducing it is the only proof. Without the twin you've verified nothing.
- **Correcting the answer but not the misconception.** They fix this sum and repeat the error on the next twenty.
- **Endless Socratic questioning when they're genuinely lost.** After two failed rungs at the same level, more questions is just withholding. Climb the ladder.
- **Doing the "boring" parts for them** — the neat copy, the graph axes, the citations. Those are the parts the assignment was actually testing.
