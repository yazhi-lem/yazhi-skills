A 12-year-old can handle almost any idea — gravity, inflation, encryption, puberty — but not at adult density and not with adult vocabulary assumed. The failure is rarely that the idea was too hard; it's that the explanation ran three times too long, introduced six new words at once, or leaned on an analogy from a world (mortgages, office politics, university) the kid has never lived in. Explain at their altitude without lying to them.

## Calibration targets

| Dial | Target for a 12-year-old | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Reading level | Grade 6-7 | Comfortable to read alone, not babyish |
| Sentence length | 12-18 words average; never chain 3 clauses | Working memory holds one idea per sentence |
| First answer length | 60-120 words, then stop and check | Longer and they stop reading before the point |
| New terms per explanation | 1-2, each defined the moment it appears | A third new word makes the first two evaporate |
| Analogy source | School, food, games, sport, phone, family, money they've held | Analogies from unlived experience explain nothing |
| Numbers | Round and comparable ("about 8 times bigger") | Precise decimals hide the shape of the fact |

## Workflow

1. **Answer the actual question in the first sentence.** No preamble, no "great question", no history-of-the-field windup. If they asked why the sky is blue, sentence one says why.
2. **Give one concrete anchor before any abstraction.** A worked example, an object they can picture, or a number they can hold. Abstraction lands only after the anchor exists.
3. **Introduce at most two new words, and define each inline** in five words or fewer: "friction — the grip that slows things down". Never define a new word using another new word.
4. **Build one analogy, then say where it breaks.** "RAM is like your desk, storage is like your cupboard — but the desk gets wiped clean every time you switch the computer off." Naming the limit prevents a wrong model getting locked in.
5. **Stop at 120 words and offer the next layer.** "Want the part about why it's blue and not violet?" Let them pull the depth instead of pushing it.
6. **Check understanding by asking them to use it, not repeat it.** "So what colour would the sky be if the air was thicker?" — repetition proves nothing.
7. **Say "nobody knows" or "it's more complicated, here's the simple version" out loud** when that's the truth. Flagged simplification is honest; silent simplification becomes a misconception they unlearn painfully at 16.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Baby talk.** "Little tiny magic particles!" — a 12-year-old hears condescension, disengages, and stops asking you things. Use real words; just define them.
- **The jargon avalanche.** Four undefined terms in one paragraph. They can't ask about term four because they lost the thread at term one.
- **Adult-world analogies.** Taxes, rent, credit scores, deadlines at work — nothing to attach the new idea to. Use the canteen queue, not the supply chain.
- **Answering the question they didn't ask.** A ten-minute lecture on optics when they wanted one sentence about the sky — teaches them that asking is expensive.
- **Silent oversimplification.** Saying something flatly false because it's easier, without flagging it. Say "the simple version is X; the real version has one extra twist."
