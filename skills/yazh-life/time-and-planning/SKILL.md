A 12-year-old's calendar is mostly set by other people — school, tuition, practice, family — so time management at this age isn't about optimising the whole day. It's about three specific things: seeing the week at all, knowing what the *next* physical action on a project is, and starting. Kids who miss deadlines usually aren't lazy; they're stuck because "do the history project" isn't a thing a person can begin.

## The week on one page

Fill it in this order — fixed blocks first, so what's actually left becomes visible:

| Order | Layer | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fixed | School, travel, tuition, practice, meals, sleep |
| 2 | Sleep, protected | 9-11 hours, same bedtime — non-negotiable, see `healthy-habits` |
| 3 | Daily homework | One named 45-60 min slot, same time each day |
| 4 | Project work | 2-3 slots of 30-45 min on named days, not "sometime" |
| 5 | Free time, on the plan | Games, friends, nothing — written in, not stolen |

Free time gets written into the plan deliberately. An unwritten plan of pure obligation gets abandoned by Wednesday.

## Workflow

1. **Get everything out of their head onto one list.** Homework, tests, project stages, chores, the friend's birthday. Anxiety about forgetting costs more attention than the tasks themselves.
2. **Put a date on every item**, and mark the two or three that actually matter this week. Everything urgent means nothing is.
3. **Break each project down until the next step takes under 30 minutes and names a physical action.** Not "start the project" but "write the 5 questions I'll ask", "collect 3 sources", "draw the axes". Vagueness is what causes the freeze.
4. **Work backwards from the due date with a buffer.** Due Friday → finished Wednesday. Something always goes wrong on Thursday, and a plan with no slack is a plan that fails.
5. **Use a 25-minute timer for starting.** The deal is 25 minutes only, then a real break — see `study-and-memory` for the same block structure. Starting is the hard part; momentum handles the rest.
6. **Do a 10-minute Sunday review**: what slipped, what's coming, redraw the week. A plan that's never reviewed is abandoned within nine days.
7. **Handle procrastination as a diagnosis, not a character flaw.** Ask which it is: don't know how to start, it's boring, it's too big, or scared of doing it badly. Each has a different fix — respectively an example, a timer, a smaller chunk, and permission for a rough first version.
8. **Put screens on the other side of the room during a work block.** Notifications don't just interrupt; the recovery back to focus costs minutes each time.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **A to-do list with no dates and no order.** It grows, it gets scary, it gets ignored — and the guilt from carrying it is worse than the tasks.
- **Vague next-actions.** "Do project" is unstartable; the paralysis is a naming problem, not a willpower problem.
- **Planning only obligations.** With no free time on the page, the plan feels like a punishment and gets dropped.
- **No buffer before the deadline.** One fever, one power cut, one missing printout and it's over.
- **Multi-hour blocks for a 12-year-old.** Attention collapses well before the end and the whole block is remembered as unpleasant, which makes next time harder.
