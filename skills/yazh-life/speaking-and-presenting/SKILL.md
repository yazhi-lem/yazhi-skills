The thing that sinks a 12-year-old's presentation is almost never the content — it's reading a script off a page in a monotone, or freezing when one sentence goes missing. Both come from the same root cause: memorising *words* instead of memorising *structure*. A kid who knows their four beats can lose any sentence and keep going.

## The five-minute talk skeleton

| Beat | Time | Job | Concrete form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 20 sec | Make them want to listen | A surprising number, a question to the room, a one-line story |
| Roadmap | 15 sec | Tell them where this goes | "Three things: what it is, why it matters, what I found" |
| Body | 3 min | Three points, one idea each | Point → example → why it matters |
| Payoff | 45 sec | The one thing to remember | "If you forget everything else, remember this:" |
| Close | 10 sec | Land cleanly | "Thank you — happy to take questions." Then stop. |

## Workflow

1. **Write the one-sentence point first.** "Plastic in the ocean gets into the fish we eat." If they can't say the whole talk in one sentence, the talk has no spine yet and slides won't give it one.
2. **Build three body points, no more.** An audience of classmates retains about three things from five minutes. A fourth point costs them one of the first three.
3. **Give every point one concrete example** — a number, an object, a photo, a short story. Abstractions alone slide off; see `age-appropriate-explaining` for choosing examples that connect.
4. **Slides get 6 words and one image, maximum.** The slide is a signpost for the audience, not the script. If a slide has sentences, they will be read aloud and the talk is dead.
5. **Rehearse out loud, standing, at least 4 times** — twice alone, once to one person, once to a small group. Silent reading through is not rehearsal; the mouth has to learn the words.
6. **Time it and cut to 90% of the limit.** A 5-minute slot needs a 4.5-minute talk; nerves speed some kids up and slow others down, and going over gets you cut off mid-sentence.
7. **Rehearse the recovery, not just the talk.** Practise saying "sorry, let me start that again", finding the place, and continuing. Knowing the recovery move is what removes the fear of blanking.
8. **Plan for questions:** prepare answers to the three most obvious ones, and practise saying "I don't know, but I'd guess…" — which is a completely acceptable answer and better than inventing.

## Handling the nerves

Nerves are the body getting ready, not a sign of failure — say that plainly. Four practical moves: slow breath out for 6 counts before starting; hold something (cards, clicker) so hands have a job; look at three friendly faces in rotation rather than scanning the whole room; and start slower than feels natural, because adrenaline makes everyone speed up. See `feelings-and-friendship` if the anxiety is bigger than stage nerves.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Reading the slides aloud.** The audience reads faster than the speaker talks, so they finish, get bored, and disengage before the sentence ends.
- **Memorising a word-for-word script.** One lost sentence collapses the whole thing, because there's no structure underneath to fall back on.
- **Eight points crammed into five minutes.** Nothing is remembered, and rushing makes the delivery worse too.
- **First rehearsal is the real thing.** Every unrehearsed talk over-runs, and the speaker discovers their own transitions live.
- **Apologising at the start** ("sorry, I didn't have much time") — it tells the room to expect something bad before they've heard anything.
