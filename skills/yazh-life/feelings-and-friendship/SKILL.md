Twelve is when friendships stop being about proximity and start being about loyalty, status, and exclusion — and when feelings arrive at an intensity nobody warned them about. Two things help most: naming the feeling accurately (which measurably lowers its intensity), and having one specific sentence to say next. Reassurance without a next move — "don't worry, it'll be fine" — reliably ends the conversation and teaches them not to bring the next problem.

## Read the situation before advising

| What's happening | Looks like | What actually helps |
|---|---|---|
| Venting | Wants to be heard, rejects every suggestion | Listen, reflect it back, ask before advising |
| Stuck on a decision | "What should I do about…" | Two or three concrete options, they choose |
| Conflict with a friend | One incident, both still care | Repair script, below |
| Bullying | Repeated, targeted, power imbalance | Not a conflict — document, block, adult, school |
| Beyond this skill | Hopeless, can't sleep or eat, self-harm, wants to disappear | Trusted adult and professional help, today |

## Workflow

1. **Name the feeling with them, precisely.** "That sounds less like angry and more like embarrassed and left out." Specific labels reduce the intensity; "upset" is too blunt to work on.
2. **Ask what they want from you before offering anything:** "Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?" Half the time the answer is listen, and advising over that need makes it worse.
3. **Separate conflict from bullying.** A fight is two people who both care, and it's repairable. Bullying is repeated, deliberate, and one-sided — it is not fixed by "sorting it out between yourselves", and telling a bullied kid to be the bigger person leaves them alone with it.
4. **Give the repair script for a friendship fight**, three sentences: name the specific thing ("when you told the group what I said…"), name the feeling ("…I felt stupid"), name the ask ("…can you not do that again?"). No "you always", no character judgements — those turn a repair into a second fight.
5. **Teach a real apology**: what I did + why it hurt + what I'll do differently. No "sorry you felt that way", which is not an apology and both people know it.
6. **Practise saying no.** "No thanks, I don't want to" is a complete sentence, and "my parents will kill me" is a perfectly good shield to use with a group. Rehearsing the words out loud is what makes them available under pressure.
7. **Give one body-level tool for the moment itself:** breathe out for longer than you breathe in, six slow rounds. It's not a metaphor — it actually slows the physical alarm enough to think.
8. **Escalate without hesitation** when you see the bottom row of the table: say plainly that this is bigger than a chat, name a specific trusted adult, and encourage contacting a local helpline or counsellor today. Never take a promise of secrecy about self-harm, and never try to be the therapist.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Jumping to solutions over a kid who wants to be heard.** They stop talking, and you lose the chance to hear the serious thing later.
- **Minimising** — "that's not a big deal", "you'll laugh about this next year". Accurate, useless, and it teaches them their feelings are wrong.
- **Treating bullying as a two-sided conflict.** It puts the responsibility on the target and leaves the pattern running.
- **Promising secrecy before hearing what it is.** If it's safety-related, the promise has to break, and breaking it costs more trust than never making it.
- **"Just ignore them."** Sometimes true, but with no other move offered it reads as "handle it alone".
