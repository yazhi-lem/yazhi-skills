Almost every 12-year-old studies by re-reading the chapter and highlighting it. That feels productive because the words get familiar — and familiarity is not memory. The two techniques that actually move exam scores, retrieval practice and spacing, both feel *worse* while you do them. Half of coaching a kid's revision is convincing them that the harder-feeling method is the one that works.

## What works, ranked

| Method | Effect | What it looks like for a 12-year-old |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | Strongest | Close the book, write everything you remember, then check |
| Spaced review | Strongest | Same topic on day 1, 3, 7, 16 — not four hours on day 0 |
| Practice questions | Strong | Past papers, end-of-chapter sums, self-made quiz cards |
| Explaining it aloud | Strong | Teach it to a parent, sibling, or an empty chair |
| Interleaving topics | Moderate | Mix three subjects in a session, don't block one for 3 hours |
| Re-reading notes | Near zero | Feels productive, builds familiarity not recall |
| Highlighting | Near zero | Colouring the textbook is not studying |

## Workflow

1. **Turn the syllabus into a question list first.** Every heading becomes a question ("What are the three states of matter?"). 20-40 questions per subject chapter. This list *is* the study material; the textbook is just where answers live.
2. **Study in 25-minute blocks with 5-minute breaks**, max 3 blocks in an evening for this age. Beyond ~90 focused minutes the returns collapse and it turns into resentment.
3. **Open every block with a brain dump.** 5 minutes, blank page, write everything remembered from last session before opening any book. The retrieval effort is where the memory forms.
4. **Schedule reviews at day 1, 3, 7, and 16** after first learning a topic. Four short reviews beat one long cram by a wide margin and survive past the test.
5. **Make the last-day session pure self-testing**, not new material. Cramming new content the night before displaces what's already consolidated.
6. **Protect sleep the night before, without exception** — see `healthy-habits`. Memory consolidates during sleep; a 4-hour night undoes a week of revision.
7. **Track what they got wrong, not what they covered.** A "keep getting this wrong" list of 5-10 items is the highest-value revision object they own.

## Making cards that work

Front asks one thing, back answers in under 15 words. "Photosynthesis?" is a bad card — it has no single answer. "What gas do plants take in during photosynthesis?" is a good one. 30 sharp cards beat 100 vague ones, and a card they can't answer twice in a row goes on the wrong-answer list instead of back in the pile.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Highlighting as a study method.** Produces a beautiful yellow textbook and no recall; the hand moved, the memory didn't.
- **The single all-nighter before the test.** Trades sleep-based consolidation for a few extra hours of low-quality input — a net loss on the day.
- **Studying with the book open.** Reading the answer while "testing" is recognition, not retrieval; the feeling of knowing is entirely fake.
- **Blocking one subject for a whole evening.** Interleaved practice is harder in the moment and clearly better on the test.
- **Cards with essay-length backs.** Nothing to retrieve cleanly, so nothing gets learnt; split into three cards.
