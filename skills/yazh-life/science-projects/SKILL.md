Most school science projects are demonstrations dressed as experiments: a baking-soda volcano proves nothing because nothing was compared. The whole skill is converting "I want to do something about plants" into "does salt water slow bean-sprout growth?" — one thing changed, everything else held still, measured with a number. That conversion is teachable in ten minutes and it's what separates a project from a craft.

## Fair test structure

| Part | Name to teach | Example: does salt water slow bean sprouts? |
|---|---|---|
| The thing you change | Independent variable | Salt: 0 g, 2 g, 5 g per litre |
| The thing you measure | Dependent variable | Height in mm on day 10 |
| Everything held still | Controlled variables | Same seeds, pot, soil, light, 50 ml water daily |
| The comparison | Control group | Plain water, 0 g salt |
| Repeats | Trials | 5 pots per group, not 1 |

## Workflow

1. **Start from something they actually noticed**, not a topic list. "Why does my brother's cycle tyre go flat faster?" is a better start than "friction".
2. **Convert it to a one-sentence testable question:** "Does X change Y?" If the question can't be answered with a measurement, it isn't ready — keep rewriting.
3. **Write the prediction down before starting**, with a reason. "I think salt will slow it because salt water dried out the road plants." Being wrong is a fine result; being wrong on record is a good project.
4. **Identify what must stay the same, out loud.** This is the step kids skip, and it's the step that makes a result mean anything.
5. **Run at least 3-5 repeats per condition.** One plant proves nothing — one seed can just be a dud. Repeats are what let them say "consistently".
6. **Measure with numbers and record as you go**, in a table with date, condition, and measurement. Filling the table from memory the night before is how good data gets lost.
7. **Do the safety check before buying anything.** See the table below; anything involving heat, mains electricity, or chemicals stronger than kitchen supplies needs an adult present, agreed in advance.
8. **Graph it, then write the conclusion in three sentences:** what happened, whether it matched the prediction, and what they'd change next time. Naming a flaw ("only 5 plants, and the window pot got more sun") earns marks — it doesn't lose them.
9. **Build the display last.** Colour and glitter add nothing if the method column is empty.

## Safety rules for home experiments

| Category | Rule |
|---|---|
| Kitchen chemistry (vinegar, soda, salt, food colour) | Fine alone; goggles if anything fizzes hard |
| Heat, flame, boiling water | Adult present, always, no exceptions |
| Mains electricity | Never. Batteries under 9 V only |
| Cleaning products | Never mix two — bleach + anything can make toxic gas |
| Anything found online | Adult reads the method first; "cool experiment" videos routinely skip the hazard |

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **The demonstration disguised as an experiment.** Nothing varied, nothing compared, so nothing was learnt — the classic volcano.
- **Changing two things at once.** More salt *and* less light: the result can't be attributed to either.
- **A single trial.** One outlier seed or one bad measurement decides the whole conclusion.
- **Backfilling the data table from memory.** Numbers get smoothed toward the prediction without anyone intending to cheat.
- **Hiding the flaw.** Kids think admitting a limitation loses marks; in every real rubric, naming it gains them.
