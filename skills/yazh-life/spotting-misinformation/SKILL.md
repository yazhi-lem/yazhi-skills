A 12-year-old encounters more claims per day than any generation before them, arriving through friends who believe them, in formats — video, screenshot, confident AI answer — that all *look* like evidence. Telling them to be sceptical achieves nothing; scepticism without a method just becomes distrust of everything, which is its own problem. Give them a routine short enough to run in thirty seconds.

## The 30-second check

Run in order, stop as soon as it fails:

| Step | Question | How |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who is saying this? | Find the original poster or site. No name, no organisation → treat as unverified |
| 2 | Do others say it too? | Search the claim in your own words; look for two independent, established sources |
| 3 | Is this the real image? | Reverse image search — old photos from other events are the most recycled lie |
| 4 | How does it want me to feel? | Rage, fear, or triumph is the sales pitch; strong emotion means slow down |
| 5 | When was it? | Check the date. Real news resurfaces years later as "breaking" |
| 6 | Is it a joke or an ad? | Satire accounts and sponsored posts get screenshotted without the label |

Step 2 has a name worth teaching: *lateral reading*. Don't study the suspicious page harder — leave it and look up the source somewhere else. Professional fact-checkers do this, and staring at the original page is what everyone else does wrong.

## Workflow

1. **Ask them what would change their mind** before debating the claim. If the answer is "nothing", the conversation is about identity, not facts, and evidence won't move it.
2. **Rank sources honestly rather than banning any.** A textbook or a government statistics site outranks a news site, which outranks a random YouTube video, which outranks a forwarded message with no source. Wikipedia is a good *starting* point — send them to its reference list, not its text, and never cite it directly in a project.
3. **Teach the AI caveat plainly:** an AI chatbot writes confident, fluent sentences whether or not the fact is real, and it invents citations that look correct. Fine for explaining an idea, not acceptable as the source in a project. Check any name, number, date, or quote elsewhere.
4. **Teach what generated images and video look like now:** hands, text on signs, jewellery, and background faces are still where it breaks — but say clearly that this is getting harder every year, so provenance beats eyeballing. "Where did this first appear?" is more reliable than "does it look fake?".
5. **Give the forwarding rule: check before sharing, every time.** Being the person who forwarded the fake thing is the actual social cost, and it's a motivation that works better than any lecture about truth.
6. **Model saying "I don't know" and "I was wrong".** A kid who has never seen an adult update a belief learns that changing your mind is a defeat.
7. **For school projects, require two independent sources per key fact**, written in a source list as they go. Retrofitting citations the night before is how uncheckable claims end up in a submitted project.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Studying the suspicious page harder** instead of leaving it to check the source elsewhere — a slick design is not evidence, and it's designed not to be.
- **"It has a photo, so it's true."** Real photo, wrong event, is the cheapest and most common manipulation there is.
- **Treating an AI answer as a source.** Confident phrasing and invented references are the specific failure mode, and citations that look real are the trap.
- **Believing it because a friend sent it.** The friend also just forwarded it; a chain of trusted people transmits a claim without anyone ever checking it.
- **Blanket cynicism.** "Everything online is fake" is as useless as believing everything, and it leaves them with no way to find out anything.
