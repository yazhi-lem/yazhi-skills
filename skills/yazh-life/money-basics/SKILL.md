Twelve is the age where money stops being abstract: there's a phone game with a shop in it, a cricket bat that costs three months of pocket money, and friends who have more. The two ideas that carry the most weight at this age are that every purchase is a trade against a *specific* other thing, and that a "free" game is free because the player is the product. Both are teachable with arithmetic they can already do.

## The split

Every rupee that arrives gets divided the moment it arrives, before any spending decision:

| Bucket | Share | Purpose | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spend | 50% | Small things, now, no permission needed | Theirs to waste; wasting is part of the lesson |
| Save | 40% | The one big thing they're aiming at | Only comes out for the named goal |
| Give | 10% | Gift, donation, someone else | Chosen by them, not by an adult |

Split first, decide later. A kid who splits ₹200 into 100/80/20 on arrival has a savings habit; a kid who "saves what's left" saves nothing, ever.

## Workflow

1. **Name the goal and put a number and a date on it.** "₹2,400 bat, ₹200/month saved, so 12 months — or 6 months if I add half of birthday money." A goal without arithmetic is a wish.
2. **Price things in units they feel, not just currency.** "This skin costs 3 weeks of pocket money" or "2 cinema trips" lands where "₹600" doesn't.
3. **Teach the trade-off question, not the ban:** "If you buy this, which of the other things you wanted are you not buying?" There's always an answer, and finding it is the skill.
4. **Use the 3-day rule for anything over one week's money.** Want it, wait three days, want it still? Buy it. This kills most impulse purchases without anyone forbidding anything, and the ones that survive were worth it.
5. **Explain how free games make money**, concretely: ads sell their attention; loot boxes are gambling odds with the odds hidden; limited-time offers manufacture panic; the first purchase is priced low on purpose. A kid who can name the tactic is much harder to run it on.
6. **Set the hard rule on digital spending: never without asking, every single time.** Saved cards and one-tap purchases exist to remove the pause where thinking happens. If a card is on the account, a spend PIN goes on it too.
7. **Introduce interest with a real number.** ₹1,000 at 6% is ₹60 a year for doing nothing — and the same 6% (or 36% on a credit card) works against you when you borrow. Both directions, same lesson.
8. **Keep a two-week spending log.** Every rupee, in a notebook or notes app. Nearly every kid is shocked by their own snack total, and the shock does more than any lecture.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Saving whatever's left at the end.** Nothing is ever left. Splitting on arrival is the entire difference.
- **Adults confiscating the "wasted" purchase money.** A ₹300 mistake at 12 is the cheapest financial lesson they will ever get; blocking it just moves the mistake to ₹30,000 at 22.
- **Banning games instead of explaining the business model.** Bans expire at 18 with no skills attached; understanding the tactic lasts.
- **A saving goal with no deadline or number.** "Saving for a laptop" never converges; "₹35,000 by next June, ₹1,500 a month plus festival money" does.
- **Comparing to what friends have.** Someone always has more; the useful comparison is against their own goal.
