A 12-year-old is online in group chats, game lobbies, and comment sections where adults also are. Blanket fear doesn't protect them and blanket bans just move the activity somewhere unsupervised. What works is a small set of concrete rules, the ability to name a manipulation tactic when it's being run on them, and — most importantly — the certainty that telling an adult will not get their device taken away.

## Never share, ever

| Category | Examples | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Full name, school name, class, uniform in photos | Turns a username into a findable person |
| Location | Home address, live location, geotagged photos, regular routes | Enables real-world contact |
| Contact | Phone number, personal email, home landline | Moves a stranger to a private channel |
| Credentials | Passwords, OTPs, PINs — including to friends | No real service ever asks; every OTP request is a scam |
| Images | Anything they wouldn't show a teacher; any photo of their body | Cannot be recalled once sent, ever |
| Money | Card numbers, UPI IDs, bank details | Also see `money-basics` |

## Workflow

1. **Set the no-blame rule first, out loud, and mean it:** "If anything online scares or confuses you, tell me. You will never be in trouble for telling me, and I won't take your device away." Fear of losing the device is the single biggest reason kids hide serious problems.
2. **Teach the grooming pattern by name**, because it is recognisable: an adult or "older kid" is unusually nice, fast; wants to move to a private chat; asks them to keep the friendship secret; sends gifts or in-game currency; asks for photos; says the parents "wouldn't understand". Any *one* of these is the moment to tell an adult. Secrecy is the tell.
3. **Give the three-second scam test:** does it create urgency, does it promise something free, does it ask for a code or a login? Any yes → don't click, don't reply, show an adult. Free skins, giveaways, "your account will be closed today", and a friend's hacked account asking for money all fail this test.
4. **Set up accounts properly once:** username with no real name or birth year, unique password per account (a password manager or three-random-words), 2FA on anything that matters, profiles private, location and "who can message me" set to friends only, DMs from strangers off.
5. **For cyberbullying, the order is: don't reply, screenshot, block, report, tell an adult.** Replying feeds it; the screenshot is what makes the school or platform able to act.
6. **Teach "screenshot means forever".** Disappearing messages don't disappear — the other person's screen is a camera. Send nothing that would be a disaster in a group chat.
7. **Agree screen rules together rather than imposing them**: no devices in the bedroom overnight (charge them outside), screens off 60 minutes before bed for sleep's sake, and one shared rule that adults follow too. Rules the kid helped set survive; see `healthy-habits`.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Confiscating the device when they report a problem.** Teaches them that honesty is punished; the next incident stays hidden until it's serious.
- **"Don't talk to strangers online."** Unusable — half of gaming is strangers. Teach the *behaviours* to watch for instead.
- **Spyware-style monitoring in place of conversation.** It ends the moment they get a second device, and it costs the trust that would have made them tell you.
- **Assuming the danger is only strangers.** Most bullying, and much pressure to share images, comes from people they know.
- **Password sharing as proof of friendship.** Extremely common at this age, and it's how accounts get taken over during the next falling-out.
