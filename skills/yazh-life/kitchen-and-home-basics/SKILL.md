A 12-year-old can cook a real meal and run a washing machine — competently, and with visible pride. What they don't yet have is the background hazard model an adult applies without noticing: that the pan handle over the edge is the accident waiting, that oil and water is the one dangerous combination, that the knife you're pushing down on with a wobbly grip is the one that slips. State the hazard *before* the step, not after, and the rest is just sequence.

## Skill ladder, in order

| Level | Cooking | Home | Supervision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold assembly: sandwich, salad, curd rice, cereal | Make bed, fold and put away clothes | None |
| 2 | Toaster, kettle, microwave; boil eggs or pasta | Sort and run laundry, hang to dry | Adult in the house |
| 3 | Stove: omelette, stir-fry, dal, upma | Iron, sew a button, change a bulb (switch off) | Adult in the house, first few times watched |
| 4 | Oven; frying in shallow oil; a full meal for the family | Unblock a sink, reset a tripped breaker | Adult present |
| Never alone | Deep frying, pressure cooker until taught, anything with mains wiring | | |

## Workflow

1. **Set the four kitchen rules before the first lesson**, once, and repeat them at the stove: pan handles turned inward; something dry to grip anything hot; a clear space to put the hot thing down *before* picking it up; and never water on an oil fire — slide a lid on and turn off the heat.
2. **Teach the knife grip properly the first time.** Sharp knife (a blunt one slips and is genuinely more dangerous), claw grip with fingertips curled back, flat side of the food down so it can't roll, and cut on a board that isn't sliding — a damp cloth underneath fixes that.
3. **Read the whole recipe before starting, and lay everything out.** The classic 12-year-old failure is discovering step 4 needs a preheated oven while step 3 is already burning.
4. **Teach the food-safety non-negotiables:** wash hands before and after raw meat or egg; separate board for raw meat; chicken cooked through, no pink; leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours; when in doubt, throw it out — a doubtful smell is not worth food poisoning.
5. **Give the clean-as-you-go rule.** Wash up during the waiting gaps. It's the difference between "cooking is fun" and "cooking means an hour of washing at the end", and it decides whether they do it again.
6. **For laundry, teach the four checks:** empty the pockets, separate darks from whites, cold wash unless the label says otherwise, and don't tumble-dry anything woollen. One red sock is a memorable lesson but an avoidable one.
7. **Agree the home-alone protocol in advance:** who to call and in what order, where the first aid kit is, how to switch the mains off, and the standing rule that no one comes in and no delivery is answered without checking with an adult first. See `first-aid-and-emergencies`.

## Anti-patterns / common failure modes

- **Water on an oil fire.** It flashes the oil into a fireball. This is the one rule to state twice and never assume they know.
- **Blunt knives "for safety".** They slide off the food instead of cutting it, straight into a finger. Sharp knife, correct grip.
- **Reading the recipe one step at a time.** The oven isn't hot, the onion isn't chopped, and something is burning during the catch-up.
- **Adults taking over at the first mess.** The mess is the price of the skill; a kid who gets displaced twice stops offering.
- **Raw chicken juice on the salad board.** The most common serious food-poisoning route in a home kitchen, and completely invisible.
- **Reaching over a hot pan** for something on the back of the stove — sleeves, steam, and spills all live there.
